# Covalent bonding

A **covalent bond** forms when two nonmetal atoms share a pair of electrons. Neither atom pulls strongly enough to take the electrons outright, so both hold on. A group of atoms joined this way is a **molecule**.

- A single bond is 1 shared pair (2 electrons).
- A double bond is 2 shared pairs.
- A triple bond is 3 shared pairs.

A **Lewis structure** shows shared pairs as lines between atoms and unshared electrons, called **lone pairs**, as dots.

To draw one:

1. Add up the valence electrons of all the atoms.
2. Connect the atoms with single bonds. The atom that can form the most bonds usually goes in the center.
3. Give the outer atoms lone pairs until each has 8 electrons. Hydrogen needs only 2.
4. Put any leftover electrons on the central atom.
5. If the central atom has fewer than 8, turn lone pairs from outer atoms into double or triple bonds.

## Worked example: water, H2O

Valence electrons: 1 + 1 + 6 = 8.

Oxygen goes in the center with a single bond to each hydrogen. That uses 4 electrons. The other 4 go on oxygen as 2 lone pairs.

Oxygen now has 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs, which is 8 electrons. Each hydrogen has 2.

## Worked example: carbon dioxide, CO2

Valence electrons: 4 + 6 + 6 = 16.

With only single bonds, carbon would have just 4 electrons. Moving one lone pair from each oxygen into the bonds gives two double bonds, and every atom has 8.

## Common mistake

Counting a shared pair for only one atom. Both atoms in a bond count the shared electrons toward their octet.
