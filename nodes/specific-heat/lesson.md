# Specific heat

**Heat** (q) is energy that flows from a warmer object to a cooler one. It is measured in joules (J). **Temperature** measures how hot something is. They are related but not the same.

**Specific heat** (c) is the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 °C.

**q = m × c × ΔT**

Here m is the mass in grams and ΔT is the temperature change, final minus initial.

| Substance | Specific heat, J/(g·°C) |
|---|---|
| Water | 4.184 |
| Aluminum | 0.897 |
| Iron | 0.449 |
| Copper | 0.385 |

Water's specific heat is unusually high. It takes a lot of energy to warm water, which is why lakes change temperature slowly.

## Worked example: warming water

How much heat is needed to warm 50.0 g of water from 20.0 °C to 45.0 °C?

ΔT = 45.0 - 20.0 = 25.0 °C

q = 50.0 g × 4.184 J/(g·°C) × 25.0 °C = **5230 J**

## Common mistake

Using the final temperature in place of ΔT. Only the change matters. If the substance cools, ΔT and q are negative, meaning heat was released.
