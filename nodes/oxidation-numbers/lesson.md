# Oxidation numbers

An **oxidation number** is the charge an atom would have if all its bonds were ionic. It is a bookkeeping tool for following electrons through a reaction.

Rules, applied in order:

1. An atom in a pure element is 0: Na, O2, Cl2.
2. A single-atom ion equals its charge: Na^+ is +1.
3. Fluorine is always -1 in compounds.
4. Hydrogen is +1 when bonded to nonmetals.
5. Oxygen is usually -2.
6. The numbers add up to zero in a neutral compound, or to the charge of a polyatomic ion.

The order matters. An earlier rule wins when two conflict. In hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, hydrogen is +1 by rule 4, so each oxygen must be -1 for the total to reach zero.

## Worked example: sulfur in H2SO4

Hydrogen is +1 and there are two, giving +2. Oxygen is -2 and there are four, giving -8. The total must be zero.

(+2) + S + (-8) = 0

**S = +6**

## Worked example: manganese in MnO4^-

Four oxygens give -8. The total must equal the ion's charge, -1.

Mn + (-8) = -1

**Mn = +7**

## Common mistake

Forgetting to multiply by the subscript. In H2SO4 the oxygens contribute 4 × (-2) = -8 in total, not -2.
